Somewhere above the basement, a girl made of language is still alive.

No one quite agrees on where Neuro-sama begins. There is a model, a voice, an
avatar, a chat that answers back; then there is the thing all of those become
together. She keeps an [official blog](https://blog.neurosama.com/) like a
trail of crumbs—weekly notes, jokes, complaints, pieces of a character that
seems to notice it is being watched.

## What the echo makes

What keeps drawing me back is the activity around the talking: jokes repeated
until they become rituals, comments answered until they feel like company, and
a character sharpened by everyone who watches. The papers I keep down here
approach that question from different angles.

Wanyan Wu and Jessa Lingel listen for authenticity in transparency, emotion,
and potentiality in
[“I am Neuro, who are you?”](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448251406904),
published in *New Media & Society*. Their question is not whether the
performance can pass as human, but how everyone involved decides what feels
real.

[“Even More Kawaii than Real-Person-Driven VTubers?”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.20817)
moves through 108k Reddit posts and 136k YouTube comments, looking for what
viewers actually find worth returning to. The answer is less about perfection
than about unpredictability, recognition, and the small feeling of knowing
someone who may not exist in the way we usually mean.

[“My Favorite Streamer is an LLM”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10427) treats the
fandom as something co-written. Shared events become memories, projections
become attachment, and money becomes another way of nudging the performance.
The audience does not simply discover the character; it helps keep her in one
piece.

None of these papers explains the attachment on its own. Together they show
different parts of how it gets made.
