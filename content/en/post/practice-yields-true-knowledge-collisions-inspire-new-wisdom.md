*Editor’s note: The “Yellow Earth” Program has run for 21 years. Over that time, more than 3,300 Zhejiang University students have taken part in administrative internships and research on innovation and entrepreneurship in counties and villages across the country. Many later chose to work at the grassroots, in central and western China, or in other roles serving the country. In 2026, more than 470 students joined the program for a one-month placement in 21 counties, cities, and districts across Zhejiang.*

*On the morning of 26 August, the 2026 Jiaojiang team held its closing ceremony at the Jiaojiang District Employment Service Center. Student representative Wang Tianyi shared his reflections after a month of grassroots practice. The full speech follows.*

Respected leaders and teachers, fellow participants in the Yellow Earth Program:

Hello, everyone. I am Wang Tianyi, a first-year student in the 2025 cohort majoring in automation at Zhejiang University. I come from Inner Mongolia. One month ago, carrying the theories I had learned from books and a curiosity about grassroots work, I arrived at the Management Committee of the Jiaojiang Economic Development Zone for the Yellow Earth Program.

Looking back on these thirty days, if I had to sum up everything I have learned in one word, I would choose “collision.” It is the collision between a strange place and my hometown, between textbook theory and the reality of grassroots work, and between youthful ideals and complex practice.

## The first collision: wind and rain in a foreign place, moonlight over home

As a student from the north, arriving on Zhejiang’s coast brought many firsts. I faced a typhoon directly and saw the damage it left across cities and villages. What moved me even more was the light burning all night in the office building of the development zone’s management committee during the storm. In that moment, I understood the weight of responsibility behind the grassroots principle of preparing for every danger even when most warnings prove unnecessary: when lives and property are at stake, every point must be defended.

When I visited villages in Zhang’an Subdistrict that had been hit by the disaster, I saw that distinctive local industries were the most solid foundation for rural communities facing natural disasters. While touring community facilities, I kept comparing what I saw with my hometown in Inner Mongolia and felt the regional development gap firsthand. Listening to stories about the spirit of land reclamation and learning about the difficulties of sharing local culture made me think of my own hometown, which also needs young people to carry its cultural inheritance forward.

During this month, I was not merely an outside visitor. I kept comparing, empathizing, and reflecting, and I planted a seed in my heart: one day, I want to bring the lessons I learned in Zhejiang back to my hometown, a place that also needs young people.

## The second collision: the optimal solution in a textbook, the people and relationships in reality

I study automation, and I am used to defining problems through models, data, and logic. Soon after I arrived, I took part in training on attracting investment in artificial intelligence. I naturally thought about industrial development in theoretical terms, but I quickly discovered that grassroots investment promotion is never a simple calculation. For a business to locate in a place and for a policy to be introduced, officials must consider ideal targets alongside business costs, fiscal risks, performance-review pressures, and other real constraints. Many decisions are not about finding a perfect optimum; they are pragmatic choices made after weighing competing interests.

As I learned how investment promotion actually works, knowledge from class such as linear regression and neural networks came to mind. Yet only when I had to handle real problems did I deeply appreciate the vast gulf between theory and practice. When I rotated to the planning and development division and faced an endless stream of residents seeking services, I gradually understood that government work involves more than cold data. It also involves human feelings, practical constraints, and the competing demands of many people. This month pushed me out of the ivory tower and taught me to view grassroots work through a more rounded and pragmatic lens.

## The third collision: youthful ideals and complex reality refined through trial and error

As a first-year student, I have ideals and enthusiasm, but I also lack practical experience. During this month I had many firsts: my first formal interview, during which I was so nervous that my voice trembled; and my first time independently coordinating an interview discussion, when poor planning and inadequate communication led to mistakes.

After making mistakes, I reviewed them seriously and learned that work requires advance planning and coordination among everyone involved. Later, drawing on articles by senior students, I gave an outreach talk to high school students in Taizhou in the hope of easing their anxiety about growing up. From being afraid to speak to standing on a stage and communicating calmly, I was pushed forward by one collision with reality after another. Little by little, they wore away my rawness and gave me a steadier outline.

The thirty days of practice passed in a flash. For me, the Yellow Earth Program was not merely an internship experience, but a profound intellectual and spiritual challenge. These collisions did not shatter my ideals; instead, they allowed my ideals to put down roots in the soil. In the future, I will carry what I saw, thought about, did, and learned during these thirty days. I will keep my feet on the ground and write the enthusiasm of youth into the places where the country needs it most.
