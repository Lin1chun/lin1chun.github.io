*Translated from the Chinese original. It was first posted on the 郁闷小屋 board of the CC98 forum on 20 September 2026.*

I can no longer remember what it felt like the last time we held hands: warm and exciting, one of the bright spots that adorned my middle-school years.

When I entered high school, I stayed behind in a small county town as academic pressure closed in. She went to the city to write her own life. We were both still childish, and we fought many times because our values did not align. Breaking up seemed to put a perfect full stop on it all. Later, when I told people about it, I could gloss over the difficult parts and let them hear a romantic story of first love.

High school was too busy. I was so busy that I buried my head in books. Countless nights, I quietly replayed every detail of our arguments, hoping I could make up for those mistakes in my next relationship. I was not nostalgic. I had realized before we broke up that I no longer loved her. I was only thinking that if there was another relationship, I had to do my very best.

I could tell that a few people in high school had crushes on me. I was no longer someone who had never dated, so why would I not notice something that simple? But I was not ready. If I dated someone as the person I was then, how would it be any different from the last time?

I would fall for someone just because we liked the same things. If we clicked, we would talk all night; if we did not, I would brush them off. When they were sad, I wanted to comfort them, but something different happened every day. I had used up all my words, and even I found my expressions of sympathy awkward. When we met, besides a few intimate gestures, we wandered silently through scenery we had long grown tired of. It felt like meeting an online friend in person. Eventually, I grew disgusted by their shallowness. It was like reading a diary I had grown bored of; I wanted to throw it away early.

Could I grow? Could I change? Probably not. So I did not dare respond to the feelings anyone showed me. I did not dare love, because I was not worthy. Apart from a lively face and a quantifiable IQ, my soul was pitifully barren. I had not read many books, and my conversation might occasionally be funny but had little substance or depth. I may have learned a little about giving people emotional support, but I did not believe I could put it to use.

Then, at last, my stifled soul was set free. On the day the exams ended, many people came to take photos with me, some I knew and some I did not. Maybe they wanted to say something, but I am sorry: I deliberately ignored part of what you were feeling. I was not going to let the excitement that might make you say those words catch fire. I am sorry that the person you met was this version of me.

In university, I grew a lot. I come from a remote town in a remote region; I had seen through the people and things there early in my life. Starting from zero helped me recover many feelings that had long gone numb. I dared to socialize openly. I could stand on a stage. I signed up for a speech. I learned to use my appearance deliberately to win a little goodwill. I could look a woman in the eye and talk to her. Maybe it was time to start a new relationship.

Was it?

The aftereffects of overthinking meant I no longer dared to do anything recklessly. On my first winter break, the journey home was 1,800 kilometers: a two-and-a-half-hour flight and a six-hour train ride. For the first time, I had a concrete sense of what it meant to live a thousand kilometers from home. Another fact then joined my future: home is far away, and poor. Going back is inconvenient and expensive.

Sitting on the train, I saw that my classmates had long since gone home. Some were already taking their parents to travel even farther away. I felt deeply that I was not one of them. I was not an ordinary person, either. I was someone whose hometown made people unconsciously prick up their ears when they heard it, someone people teased for leaving home so far behind, someone who could not get home immediately when he missed it.

I am not especially intelligent, but I have spent enough time proving that I am not stupid. I can communicate with local people, learn the common knowledge they already have, and most likely become as well-off as the classmates around me after graduation: first on my own, then, if I am lucky, with a second person, and, if I work a little harder, perhaps a third.

But was that enough? Was that my qualification to start a relationship? Of course, if I fell in love with someone else from a distant place, I would accept that supposed flaw without hesitation. Love can cross mountains and seas. That is not the problem.

But considering the position of another person made one thing clear: that does not give them a reason to accept it. There might be someone who loves me to the bone and accepts every shortcoming in me, even my coming from somewhere so far away. But there are many people like me, and I am the one among them with the biggest flaws. I cannot convince myself that I have any advantage over other people. You could call it an inferiority complex, but first of all it is a clear-eyed view of myself.

You will stay in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shanghai, in the south, in developed regions, growing freely. My soul carries the color of the underdeveloped north, and I cannot wipe it away. I am sorry. You cannot like me. You cannot fall in love with me. I should not be liked by you, and I should not be loved.

Maybe after I find a job, I will have a firmer, more confident answer to this question. I do not know. I only know that, at this stage of university, I still do not deserve a relationship.
