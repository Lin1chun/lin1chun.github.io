As a first-year student majoring in automation, I once held “using technology to liberate human labor” as my ideal. It was only after reading the *Communist Manifesto* that I realized my pursuit bore a dangerous resemblance to the logic Marx criticized: both sought to “separate” people from labor.

Marx exposed how capital, through the division of labor and wage employment, alienates people into “commodities of labor power.” In the future I imagined, automation would make this “commodity” disappear altogether. I had assumed that abolishing labor would end alienation, but the *Communist Manifesto* awakened me to a different truth: alienation is rooted not in labor itself, but in the ownership and organization of production behind it. If the automation I envisioned did not change social relations, it might only deprive people of labor—the very path through which they realize themselves. Automation may reshape the structure of society: at one end stands a “technology–capital complex” that controls algorithms, data, and the means of production; at the other, a vast population excluded from production. When labor power is no longer an essential “resource” for the economic system, traditional class narratives may face a fundamental challenge. If automation follows only the one-way logic of capital and efficiency, without building a social system that upholds human value and dignity, it could produce a technology-driven, large-scale, systemic exclusion.

The communist ideal is to reshape labor within a “community of free individuals,” making it a manifestation of humanity’s essential powers rather than a forced means of subsistence. This made me realize that true liberation may lie not in “leaving labor behind,” but in using technological progress to break the constraints of necessary labor time, so that everyone can become more complete through freely creative activity—as Marx put it, “allowing me to do this today and that tomorrow according to my own inclinations.”

This requires our generation of technologists to take on corresponding responsibilities while pursuing efficiency: to ask whom technology serves, who directs it, and how its fruits are shared, and to attend to the plight of those who may be “optimized away” by technology. A robot can interpret the *Communist Manifesto*, but it will never dream of it. With clear minds and fiery hearts, we must create a future in which technology serves human liberation rather than replacing people.
